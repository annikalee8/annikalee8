LUMA
HEALTH.

International strategy / Market opportunity / Digital health / Product concept

A Germany-focused concept for a wearable health service combining continuous tracking, physician connectivity and AI-supported insights.

Team project by Max Cutler, Annika Lee and Piper Toney. LUMA Health is a hypothetical academic concept; pricing, costs, adoption targets and projections shown here were developed for coursework, not operating results.

The opportunity

Start with a market tension, not a product idea.

The project began by studying Germany's macro environment. The team's PESTEL work highlighted three forces that could create space for a digital-health concept: an aging population, a shortage of skilled healthcare workers and strong national support for digitalization.

Market context

The need was both demographic and operational.

The team's analysis connected rising care needs with the practical challenge of delivering healthcare when staffing is constrained. Rather than framing technology as novelty, the concept positioned digital monitoring as a way to make existing healthcare capacity more accessible and continuous.

The concept

Connect the wearable, the physician and the intelligence layer.

LUMA Health was designed as more than a fitness tracker. The concept combined basic health monitoring with a connection to primary care and an AI service that could analyze information, surface potential issues and support earlier intervention.

01

Track

Monitor signals such as heart rate, blood pressure, sleep, activity and falls.

02

Connect

Give the primary-care physician a more continuous picture of the user's health and allow additional medical information to inform monitoring.

03

Interpret

Use an AI-supported layer to analyze inputs, provide guidance and flag potential health issues earlier.

04

Access

Reduce the need for unnecessary travel and make health support more practical for older and rural users.

Industry analysis

A promising market still needs a reason to choose you.

The Five Forces analysis did not assume that demand alone would make the concept attractive. The team identified high substitute pressure and high competitive rivalry, with established healthcare and technology players already active in adjacent spaces. That shifted the strategy toward differentiation, trust and healthcare partnerships rather than simple first-mover logic.

Target market

Design around people with the strongest access and monitoring needs.

The proposed market focus centered on older adults, people with pre-existing conditions and rural communities with more limited access to medical care. The project also considered income because the proposed wearable and service model carried a premium price.

Differentiation

Make the value proposition visible in the service, interface and care experience.

The concept's differentiation was built across three dimensions: a more connected service, an approachable wearable design and a more personalized information flow. The goal was to make the product useful to people who might not identify as early adopters of technology.

Roadmap

Sequence regulation, pilots and partnerships before scale.

The five-year roadmap started with regulatory and GDPR approvals, an MVP and pilot, then moved toward launch in major cities, insurer and clinic integration, nationwide availability and eventual expansion beyond Germany. The sequencing reflects how healthcare concepts depend on trust, compliance and institutional relationships as much as product development.

Risks

For health technology, trust and adoption are part of the product.

The team explicitly considered risks beyond technical feasibility. Privacy regulation, established competitors and reluctance among some seniors to adopt unfamiliar technology each required a different response: compliance expertise, sharper differentiation and hands-on human support.

Business model

Test a hybrid model across consumer, provider and device revenue.

The academic model explored several revenue streams rather than relying on the wearable alone: consultations, a consumer subscription, provider SaaS, insurance reimbursement and the device purchase itself. The proposed prices were benchmarked against telehealth and wearable competitors in the German market.

Takeaway
A market opportunity is stronger when the environment changes the product — not when the research is only used to justify it.

The most useful part of this project was connecting macro research to concrete product decisions. Germany's aging population shaped the target user, the labor shortage strengthened the case for remote monitoring, digital-health policy informed the delivery model, and privacy requirements affected the operating strategy.
