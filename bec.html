BODY ENERGY
CLUB.

Market research / Consumer insights / Pricing / Promotion

A student-focused research project examining how Body Energy Club's UBC location could improve awareness, product mix and promotional relevance.

Team university project. Findings and recommendations were developed from primary and secondary research; sales and revenue figures were not disclosed by the business.

The question

How could a health retailer become more relevant to students without relying on smoothies alone?

Body Energy Club's UBC location was highly associated with smoothies and smoothie bowls, but the project identified a gap between what students recognized and which categories were potentially more attractive from a margin and shelf-life perspective. The research focused on product awareness, willingness to pay and the promotional tactics most likely to resonate with students.

Research design

Use multiple methods to separate perception from behavior.

The team combined qualitative and quantitative methods so that pricing, awareness and product perceptions could be examined from several angles. Student responses were also segmented by activity level to test whether a more health- or fitness-oriented lifestyle changed willingness to pay.

01

Manager interview

Understand category economics, perishability and the operational context behind what sells.

02

Student surveys

Measure awareness, purchasing behavior, willingness to pay and reactions to promotional ideas.

03

Focus groups

Explore why students found the brand appealing, expensive, unfamiliar or intimidating.

04

Statistical testing

Use chi-square analysis to test whether willingness to pay differed between active and non-active students.

Finding 01

The most visible products were not necessarily the most strategically valuable.

Students who had purchased from BEC most often reported buying smoothies or smoothie bowls. At the same time, the manager interview and secondary research highlighted the lower margins and perishability associated with fresh made-to-order products, while supplements offered longer shelf life and stronger profit potential. That created a visibility problem: the category the team wanted students to notice more was not the category they most associated with the brand.

Finding 02

Price sensitivity was not uniform across the student market.

The team compared active and non-active students rather than treating the campus as one audience. Chi-square testing indicated statistically significant relationships between activity level and willingness to pay for smoothies, protein shakes and supplements/vitamins, while the smoothie-bowl result did not meet the 5% significance threshold. That supported a more segmented approach to pricing and promotion.

Finding 03

Awareness and approachability mattered as much as the discount itself.

Participants responded to several promotional ideas, including a percentage discount, loyalty stamp card, in-person sampling and influencer content. Focus-group feedback also pointed to convenience, visible ingredients, seasonal offers and simpler entry points for students who felt the brand or menu was difficult to approach.

Recommendations

Turn the research into changes in place, promotion and customer relationship.

The final recommendations aimed to make higher-margin products more visible, reduce the barrier to trial and build a stronger relationship with students over time. The plan combined in-store merchandising with campus activation, loyalty, CRM and a more approachable brand presence.

01

Rework product visibility

Move supplements into higher-traffic areas so the broader assortment is easier to discover.

02

Sample where students already are

Use gyms, recreation spaces and campus events to lower the friction of first trial.

03

Strengthen loyalty

Pair student price incentives with a points-based loyalty structure and customer-preference data.

04

Build campus presence

Use ambassadors, social media and simpler educational messaging to make the brand feel easier to enter.

Research limitations

Be explicit about what the data could and could not support.

The presentation documented incomplete survey responses, sample-size limitations, limited secondary data, confidentiality around actual sales and revenue, and the fact that findings were specific to the UBC location. The proposed next steps included a larger sample, more in-person data collection and better alignment with broader brand goals.

Takeaway
Good market research is useful when it changes who you target, what you emphasize and where you show up.

This project connected qualitative insight, statistical testing and competitor benchmarking to concrete marketing decisions. The most useful shift was moving away from treating “UBC students” as one audience and toward a more segmented view of price sensitivity, familiarity and product interest.
